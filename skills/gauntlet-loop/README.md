# gauntlet-loop — design notes and decisions

`SKILL.md` is what Claude loads; `references/` is what the lead reads when it runs. This file is for humans: the decision behind every place the skill could have gone another way. Each one names the alternative, why this one, what it costs, and the evidence — so a later change is a change of mind, not a drift.

Evidence cited by title is the LLM-as-judge literature these choices lean on: Zheng et al. 2023, *Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena* (position bias, verbosity bias, self-enhancement bias; pairwise beats absolute grading); Wang et al. 2023, *Large Language Models are not Fair Evaluators* (swapping candidate order flips verdicts; evaluate both orders); Panickssery et al. 2024, *LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations*; Verga et al. 2024, *Replacing Judges with Juries* (judges from different model families are less biased than one judge); Huang et al. 2023, *Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet*. Harness facts were checked against the Claude Code docs in August 2026.

## Decisions

**D1 — One short prompt, not a method document.** Alternative: a method the agent follows plus a long orchestrator prompt with numbered steps. The method's own first rule is "give it the goal, not your implementation"; a prompt that prescribes decomposition and procedure replaces the agent's judgment with the author's. The paste prompt states the whole loop in about 230 words and leaves every other decision to the lead. Cost: a session without the skill installed has less to go on — accepted, because the prompt is complete in itself and the skill exists for the rest.

**D2 — The exit is a confirmed win or the user; never "no improvement in N rounds", never a round count.** Alternative: stop when two rounds bring nothing, or after a budget of rounds. A plateau is the signal to split the piece or change builders — the escalation ladder (D9) exists for exactly that — and a "no improvement" exit is the lead grading its own progress. Cost: an unattended run against a bar it cannot reach spends until the user stops it, which is why D3 prefers reachable bars and a user-named budget goes into the `/goal` line as a second exit. This is the one decision taken on principle rather than evidence: cost is bounded by the user and the bar, not by a rule.

**D3 — Prefer the hardest bar the agent can genuinely reach; an out-of-reach bar only when the user wants the pull.** Alternative: always aim past reach. An unreachable bar pulls the work further and is stopped by hand; a reachable one gives a defined exit and a result at least as good as a real thing. Both are legitimate. The skill offers both and says which is which, because under `/goal` the choice decides who ends the run.

**D4 — Pairwise pick for taste; command floors for everything objective; no absolute scores.** Alternative: PASS/FAIL or a score per criterion, with "default to FAIL" as the correction for leniency. Pairwise judgments are more consistent than scores (Zheng et al.), and scores drift upward across rounds as the judge anchors on its last one. What is objective — tests, contrast, false positives, a citation opening — is not an opinion, so it is run by command before any critic. The split is principled: absolute where a command decides, pairwise where taste decides. The pairwise equivalent of "default to FAIL" is "a tie is a loss for ours", which the prompt carries.

**D5 — Observations before the pick; one gap, stated as an observation, never as a fix.** Alternative: a list of every defect with prescribed fixes. A judge that picks first writes observations to fit; reasoning-first improves agreement (Zheng et al.). One gap, because ten gaps get ten shallow patches and no re-think. No fixes, because a fix is one critic's prescription — the next critic would prescribe differently — and a builder handed fixes optimises for critic taste instead of the bar; it is "give the goal, not the implementation" applied to the critic. Cost: a piece with several orthogonal defects surfaces them one round at a time; splitting a quality dimension into its own piece (D6) is the remedy when that is too slow.

**D6 — One critic per verdict, not a panel of lenses.** Alternative: several critics per round, one lens each (correctness, performance, aesthetics, accessibility), votes combined. The honest case for a panel is that identical judges share blind spots (Verga et al.). The skill gets that diversity without a panel because each lens already has a better home: objective lenses are command floors; reading lenses are the FLOOR lines every critic checks on both sides; a taste dimension that deserves its own judgment becomes its own piece with its own loop; and model diversity — the kind the evidence actually shows helps — comes from the confirming critic running on a different model. What a panel would add is a vote on the pick, at N critics per round, for a gain that only matters in the losing case, where a wrong verdict costs one round. The second judgment is spent where a wrong verdict is expensive instead (D7). Where a panel would still win: a single piece whose taste dimensions cannot be split — rare, and the assembled-whole gate catches what per-piece verdicts miss.

**D7 — Confirm wins only, with the order swapped and a different model; no minimum rounds.** Alternative: a minimum round count before a pass counts, on the theory that the first pass is often a lenient critic. The two errors are asymmetric: a false win ends the loop early, a false loss costs one more round. So the second critic is spent on wins. The swap is the balanced-position check Wang et al. propose; a new coin flip repeats the first order half the time. The different model breaks self-preference (Panickssery et al.): against a human-made reference, a same-model critic is a thumb on ours. A minimum-round rule names the same problem but wastes rounds when the work is good and does not fix a lenient critic — it passes again.

**D8 — Blind by procedure.** Alternative: tell the critic the reference by name and pass the builder's notes along. The lead fetches and freezes; the critic never hears the reference's name (a critic told "Nike" finds the swoosh), never sees builder notes (it anchors on the justification and agrees — the self-review failure in Huang et al.), never sees history or which side changed (a reused critic knows which side is ours), and is never named after a piece, round or role (every subagent sees the session's roster). Builders return one line — the path — so nothing leaks forward and the lead's context stays small over a long run. Residual risk: a famous reference can be recognised from its pixels or its voice; the answer is to compare pieces rather than whole pages, and to prefer the less iconic of two equally hard references.

**D9 — The builder keeps its context while the gap changes; a repeated gap escalates.** Alternative: a clean-context builder every round. Continuity is cheaper than re-briefing, and a builder that remembers its last change closes the next gap faster. The moment it stops working — the same gap twice — is also the signal that the builder's frame is the problem, so the ladder is: split the piece on that gap, retire the builder for a fresh one given only the artifact and the gap, then three variants each with a different one-line approach. A clean context every round throws away the good case to avoid the bad one.

**D10 — Smoothing once, at the whole gate, then the whole is judged.** Alternative: a smoothing pass after each wave, or after convergence with no judgment after it. Mid-run smoothing edits pieces that are still moving and silently invalidates confirmed wins. Smoothing once, re-running the floors, then judging the assembled whole against the whole reference — with a loss becoming a new "coherence" piece — keeps every confirmation honest. Cost: seams are found late. Accepted; the gate loops until the whole wins.

**D11 — `/goal` leads the prompt; `/loop` is the fallback.** Alternative: a `/loop until …` line inside the prompt. That line is plain text — a slash command counts only as the first token — so the lead would have to invoke the loop skill and reschedule every turn; the same model that runs the loop could end it (`stop: true`), the builder grading itself one level up; forgetting twice kills the run silently; every wakeup waits at least a minute, on a task that expires in seven days. `/goal` moves the loop into the harness: the whole prompt is the condition (up to 4,000 characters), a separate small model judges it after every turn from the transcript, the next turn starts at once, evaluation waits while subagents run, it survives forgetting, returns on `--resume`, and only the user clears it. Two adaptations: the lead ends every turn with the count of confirmed pieces (the evaluator cannot run commands) and never writes that the bar is out of reach (the evaluator can clear a goal it judges impossible). The residual risk, shared with `/loop`, is a lead that claims "all confirmed" without the verdict files behind it — the swapped second critic and the saved verdicts are what make the claim auditable. `/loop` remains as the first token where `/goal` is unavailable (hooks disabled), and in run mode when the user says "run it" in-session; never mid-prompt.

**D12 — A harder bar is a new run.** Alternative: raise the bar mid-run once the first one is met. Raising it invalidates every confirmed win and, under `/goal`, changes nothing in the condition the evaluator is judging. Finish, then start again with the result as the starting artifact.

**D13 — Examples are verdicts in the required shape, not narrative runs.** Alternative: long worked example runs in prose. A run in prose teaches by imitation, and what gets imitated is its shape — a critic that grades and prescribes, a builder that explains itself — which is exactly what D4–D8 forbid. What ships instead: a verdict in the exact required shape at the end of every domain file, a workbench snapshot in `references/running-the-loop.md`, and one end-to-end run in the skill's own shape, `references/example-run.md` - a red floor, a filled critic prompt, a loss routed as one gap, a discarded verdict, a swapped confirmation on another model, a repeated gap split, a compaction turn, the whole gate lost on coherence and won. A trace in the right shape teaches the right shape by imitation.

**D14 — The lead writes the tests, eval sets and held-out material; no critic co-authors a floor.** Alternative: the critic writes or keeps the tests. A critic that wrote the tests judges with a stake in them, and since critics are fresh every round, "the critic keeps the held-out set" cannot even be carried out. The lead never picks a winner, so holding the floors is not a conflict; a floor the lead wrote wrong is corrected once, logged, and every confirmation it touched is re-run.

## Cost

A run is dozens of agents: every round is a builder turn plus a critic, a win adds a confirming critic, an escalation adds three builders, and the whole gate adds a smoother and two more critics. Five pieces over four rounds is on the order of forty to fifty agent invocations before the gate. That is the price of the method; the skill adds no default cap because the user names the budget or ends the run.
